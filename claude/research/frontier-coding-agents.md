# How leading firms run coding agents

*Research summary, 2 November 2026, compiled with Claude from public sources. Most figures are self-reported by the companies and haven't been audited, and many come secondhand, through press coverage, conference talks or relays of executives' posts.*

## Bottom line

At the companies furthest along, more and more coding work runs through background agents. They work in sandboxes that look like an engineer's dev environment, CI checks their output, and a person usually reviews the pull request before it merges. These companies do orchestrate, sometimes heavily, but they fit it to the job, and most keep a single agent writing the code for each task.

At some of them, agents now produce most of the code. Ramp credits its agent with 74% of merged pull requests, and Anthropic says Claude writes over 80% of its merged code. Elsewhere the share is smaller: agents created 35% of merged PRs at Cursor and 11% at Uber. Stripe merges more than 1,300 agent-written PRs a week but hasn't said what share of the total that is. These numbers show adoption. They say little about quality, and the figures that would (revert rates, escaped defects, total cost) are mostly unpublished.

What these teams share is heavy investment in the environment: realistic sandboxes, checks that finish in seconds, and review at merge. They add structure around the agent where it protects intent, handles real uncertainty or coordinates independent pieces of work. Stripe's blueprints, Airbnb's migration pipeline and OpenAI's Symphony, which gives each ticket its own agent, are all examples. Splitting the writing itself across agents is less common. Cognition, which once argued against multi-agent systems altogether, now says they work best when one agent writes and the others research or review. Cursor's swarms do have hundreds of agents writing in parallel, mostly on new projects with near-perfect test suites, and Cursor credits the gains to isolating context and removing complexity.

The teams that describe how they plan, including OpenAI, Anthropic and Spotify, use short plans and treat passing tests as the definition of done. None of the firms profiled here reports using a spec-driven development (SDD) framework. The evidence on those frameworks is thin and mixed. Practitioner tests found that Spec Kit multiplied review time, and a small study found that specs didn't help reviewers catch more bugs, though they made problems easier to trace back to requirements. The one benchmark showing fewer defects with spec tools comes from a vendor and hasn't been independently checked.

Large banks have given coding assistants to tens of thousands of developers, but few describe their controls in detail. JPMorgan has talked about an isolated environment and spending caps, Goldman says its Devin pilot runs under human supervision, and Capital One keeps humans in the loop for high-risk decisions. Regulators have started asking banks about agent permissions, audit trails, oversight and shutdown, though they aren't yet prescribing specific controls.

## The common pattern

Most of the leaders have settled on the same basic shape. One agent takes one task, works in the same environment an engineer would use, checks its own work, and hands a pull request to a person.

1. Work starts where it already lives. Engineers kick off agents from Slack, a ticket or a web page (Stripe, Ramp, Spotify, Shopify, Linear).
2. The agent runs in a real dev environment. Sandboxes are pre-warmed, hold the full stack and can't reach production. Stripe reuses the devboxes its engineers work in, and Uber's agent platform grew out of its remote dev environments.
3. One agent writes the code and tests it. It edits, runs lint and tests, and fixes whatever fails, while fixed steps like linting, pushing and CI run around it. Stripe caps this at two CI rounds.
4. A person approves the merge. At Stripe, the engineer who launched the agent opens the PR and asks a colleague to review it. Ramp reportedly credits each PR to the person who asked for it, so they can't approve it themselves. Low-risk work is the exception: Spotify auto-merges safe fleet changes and lets migration drivers approve their own PRs, and OpenAI automerges small cleanup PRs.

A few ideas sit behind that shape.

- Keep things as simple as the work allows. Anthropic recommends the [simplest solution that works](https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-effective-agents). Cognition now says multi-agent setups work best when a single agent writes and the rest research or review, and Cursor credits many of its gains to [removing complexity](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents). Most parallelism comes from running many independent sessions, one per task.
- Harnesses get smaller as models improve. Anthropic strips parts out of its harnesses as models get better, Spotify reportedly dropped its LLM judge, and in one small study a spec helped a weak model far more than a strong one.
- Context stays short and sits in the repo. AGENTS.md works as a map, Stripe scopes its rules by directory, and Cloudflare generated AGENTS.md files for about 3,900 repositories.
- Review has become the constraint. OpenAI says the limit is human attention, Ramp says it's judgment about what to ship, and Spotify now has 76% more PRs to review. That's pushing money into verification: AI reviewers at Uber, Cloudflare and Cursor, hidden verifiers at Spotify and real-browser checks at Ramp.
- Pipelines suit fleet work. State-machine pipelines show up where the work is mechanical and easy to check, like Airbnb's test migration and Spotify's Fleet Management. The big multi-agent "swarm" successes were mostly new projects with near-perfect test suites, which existing codebases rarely have.

## Where orchestration helps

The pipelines that work leave the mechanical steps to ordinary code and give one capable agent room to reason. In each case, a pass/fail check decides when work moves forward.

| Pipeline | Unit of work | Handled by code | Handled by the agent | Gate and cap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Stripe Minions](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2) | One task or ticket | Linting, autofixes, git push and CI runs | Implementing the task and fixing CI failures | A local lint loop, then at most two CI rounds before a person takes over |
| [Airbnb test migration](https://medium.com/airbnb-engineering/accelerating-large-scale-test-migration-with-llms-9565c208023b) | One test file | The sequence of steps (refactor, Jest, lint, TypeScript), lint autofix, and gathering up to 50 related files as context | Rewriting the file and fixing each step's errors | A file only advances when its step passes. Each step has a retry limit, and the hardest files took 50–100 attempts |
| [Spotify Honk](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/12/feedback-loops-background-coding-agents-part-3) | One change in one repo | Choosing target repos and opening PRs through Fleet Management, and running the verifiers | Making the code change | Verifiers sit behind a single tool, and a stop hook blocks the PR until they pass |

Airbnb's results show why this suits mechanical work. Three quarters of the files passed in the first four-hour run, 97% passed after four days of tuning against sampled failures, and engineers finished the last 3% by hand. The team found that brute-force retries and picking the right context files mattered more than polishing the prompt.

In all three, what moves from one step to the next is the code itself, and a check decides whether it goes on. The only handoff between agents is at Spotify, where an intake agent talks the task through with the engineer and then writes the coding agent's prompt.

The AI labs orchestrate too. OpenAI's [Symphony](https://openai.com/index/open-source-codex-orchestration-symphony) gives each open Linear issue its own Codex agent and restarts any that crash or stall. It uses Linear's ticket statuses as its state machine, but OpenAI writes that "treating agents as rigid nodes in a state machine doesn't work well," and that the better approach is to "give them tools and context and let them cook." OpenAI also describes a nine-stage pipeline in which agents review code, shepherd approved changes through deployment and watch production, as well as writing the code ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/openai-software-factory)). Anthropic's [harness for long-running app builds](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps) has a planner turn a short prompt into a spec, a generator build it and a separate evaluator test the running app. In one test, a solo run cost $9 and left the core feature broken, while the full harness took six hours and $200 and produced a playable game.

It helps to think of the system as doing three separate jobs:

1. Preserving intent: what to build and why, the invariants, and what success looks like. Plans, specs and tests carry this.
2. Choosing how to execute, which should adapt to how ambiguous, risky and large the task is.
3. Verifying the result and deciding who can accept it, through repeatable checks plus independent or human sign-off where it matters.

A companion note, [Structured handoffs and approval gates](agent-handoffs-and-gates.md), looks at what these systems actually pass between stages, which transitions need a person, and what the frameworks offer for both.

## Where spec-driven development fits

Writing down what you want before an agent starts coding is standard practice. Mandatory, heavyweight SDD frameworks are another matter. The label covers three quite different things: durable intent and acceptance criteria, lightweight plans or spec deltas for a single task, and persona-based multi-stage frameworks like BMAD. The evidence against is strongest for the last of these, and the frameworks themselves have been getting lighter.

| Tool | Approach | Status, Nov 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [GitHub Spec Kit](https://github.com/github/spec-kit) | Spec, plan and task templates that work with many agents | Reached v1.0 in Aug 2026 and has about 143k GitHub stars. Thoughtworks rates it "Assess" |
| [Kiro](https://kiro.dev/blog/one-year/) (AWS) | An IDE that writes requirements, design and task specs | More than 250k developers used it in its first three months ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2025/amazons-surprise-indie-hit-kiro-launches-broadly-in-bid-to-reshape-ai-powered-software-development/)). It has since added a quick mode without approval gates, and its 2026 launches have focused on agent-platform features |
| [OpenSpec](https://github.com/Fission-AI/OpenSpec) | Proposals and spec deltas for each change | v1.0 (Jan 2026) swapped its fixed sequence for flexible actions. About 73k stars, rated "Assess" |
| [BMAD Method](https://github.com/bmad-code-org/BMAD-METHOD) | A persona-based agile workflow with an analyst, PM, architect and developer | v7 is still in alpha, the core skills went from 14 to 8, and Thoughtworks calls it more rigid than OpenSpec |
| [Tessl](https://tessl.io/) | Specs as the source of truth, with a spec registry | The spec framework no longer appears on its site, which now leads with agent skills |

Practitioner tests point to a big review cost. In one comparison, a Spec Kit feature produced 2,577 lines of Markdown and took about 3.5 hours to review, while the same goal with plain prompting took about 24 minutes of review, testing and fixes ([Scott Logic](https://blog.scottlogic.com/2025/11/26/putting-spec-kit-through-its-paces-radical-idea-or-reinvented-waterfall.html)). Another test produced 1,300 lines of text for a small date-display feature ([Marmelab](https://marmelab.com/blog/2025/11/12/spec-driven-development-waterfall-strikes-back.html)).

A small study, which its author calls preliminary, found that giving reviewers a spec didn't help them find more bugs (recall of 0.525 versus 0.518) and stretched review from 27 to 48 minutes. It did help them trace problems back to requirements, with attribution rising from 0.00 to 0.81. In generation tests, a spec lifted a weak model by about 21 points and a strong one by about 2. The weak-model result rests on four runs and adds an extra generation pass, and in a separate test, asking the model to reason before coding did about as well as asking for a spec ([InfoQ, Sep 2026](https://www.infoq.com/articles/when-spec-driven-development-pays-off/)).

A benchmark from Uvik, a software staffing firm, ran 50 brownfield Python tickets once through each tool and found fewer defects per merged ticket with every spec tool (0.39 to 0.57, against 0.86 with no spec). The no-spec runs merged fewer tickets but were the fastest, and cheaper than every tool except Kiro. Uvik says its numbers haven't been independently verified, puts its own noise at about ±10 points on merge rate, and gives conflicting dates for the run ([Uvik](https://uvik.net/blog/spec-driven-development-benchmark/)).

Two independent studies look at specs in the wild. One, covering 88,052 pull requests, found that spec artifacts didn't reduce defects, though it studied linked tickets rather than output from SDD tools ([ESEM 2026](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ESEM.2026.84)). The other counted 470,795 spec files across 73,030 public repos and found that 81.5% were committed only once and 98.3% had a single author. That suggests most specs are written once and left alone, although 92% were first committed in 2026, so many are simply new ([SpecMine, Oct 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25202)).

Company reports are uncontrolled but still useful. Asana tried Spec Kit and OpenSpec and neither stuck, but a lighter spec skill it built in-house did, and its seven heaviest users merged 38% more PRs a week ([Asana](https://asana.com/inside-asana/spec-driven-development)). So specs can help in the right form. No large company has published controlled results from any SDD framework, although some may be using one without saying so.

Analysts are cautious as well. Thoughtworks rates Spec Kit and OpenSpec "Assess," while [AGENTS.md](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques/agents-md) and [Agent Skills](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques/agent-skills) sit one ring closer to adoption at "Trial."

Leading teams lean on lighter tools instead. OpenAI's Codex team keeps its AGENTS.md to about 100 lines, calling it "a map, not a 1,000-page instruction manual," and checks design docs and plans into the repo, with lightweight plans for small changes ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)). OpenAI's [ExecPlans](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/articles/codex_exec_plans) are a single living plan document for complex features and big refactors, and Anthropic's long-running agents track a JSON feature list with pass/fail flags alongside a progress file ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)). Claude Code's [best-practices guide](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices) recommends a short CLAUDE.md and a failing test for the agent to turn green, and for bigger features suggests having Claude interview you and write a SPEC.md before building in a fresh session. Its [plan mode](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/permission-modes) has Claude research and propose changes before it edits anything. Spotify writes migration prompts as end states that tests can verify ([Spotify](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/11/context-engineering-background-coding-agents-part-2)).

The teams that describe their practice keep the useful core of SDD: a short plan and tests that define done. More structure may well be worth it for ambiguous or high-stakes work, but nobody has tested that properly either. For now the evidence doesn't justify making heavyweight SDD mandatory for all work, and the little evidence there is suggests specs matter less as models get stronger.

## Implications for regulated enterprises

Designs that lean heavily on orchestration, with a state machine coordinating separate agent sessions behind spec frameworks and rules-engine gates, put controls in sensible places at the edges. The open question is the middle. Among the leaders, what passes between stages is usually the code, checked by a test, or occasionally a single prompt or spec, and one agent writes each task. A chain of documents and instructions relayed from one agent session to the next is where these designs differ most.

| Design choice | What the leaders do | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deterministic steps around agents | Stripe's blueprints, Airbnb's per-file pipeline, Shopify's Roast and Spotify's verifiers all wrap a single agent in fixed steps | Matches the leaders, as long as each task gets one agent |
| A separate agent session for each stage, passing structured outputs along | Usually one agent writes each task. Where work is split, stages hand over code checked by tests (Stripe, Airbnb) or a single prompt or spec (Spotify's intake agent, Anthropic's planner) | Differs from the common case. Cognition and GitHub both report that handoffs lose context, with subagents redoing work or assuming things the others don't know. None of the systems we looked at documents a schema check on what one agent hands the next |
| SDD frameworks as the entry point | Where teams describe it (OpenAI, Anthropic, Spotify), a short AGENTS.md, plans in the repo, and tests as the definition of done | Differs. Practitioner tests found a large review cost, and no independent study has shown fewer defects |
| Rules engines deciding whether to pass, retry or escalate | CI, linters and stop hooks inside the loop, with human review at merge. Spotify reportedly dropped its LLM judge, which had become too rigid and blocked valid changes | Most useful at merge, deploy and data access. No leader reports a rules engine between reasoning steps, and OpenAI found that treating agents as rigid state-machine nodes didn't work well |
| Evidence collected at every step | PR history, CI logs and session transcripts. Shopify's River only works in public Slack channels, so every session leaves a searchable transcript | Same goal. Regulators ask about audit trails, and FINRA recommends logging agent actions, prompts and outputs |
| A central platform driven from a CLI | Background agents started from Slack, tickets or the web, running in the same environments engineers use | Partly. A central platform makes sense, but a pipeline someone drives from a CLI keeps that person supervising it. OpenAI built Symphony after finding engineers could only manage three to five sessions at once |

The research points to these priorities:

1. Build an agent-ready sandbox. Give agents the environment engineers already use, cut off from production and personal credentials, the way Stripe's devboxes and JPMorgan's Devspace are.
2. Make feedback fast inside the loop, with lint and test results in seconds, a hook that won't let the agent call itself done until checks pass, and a cap on CI rounds.
3. Put gates at the boundaries. That means human review at merge with separation of duties (GitHub blocks the requester from approving Copilot's PRs, and at Stripe the engineer who launched the agent asks a colleague to review), plus tight controls on deploys and data access, tiered by risk. Gates should fail closed, and each approval should be tied to the exact action approved, since a resumed run can repeat it.
4. Keep context short and in the repo, with AGENTS.md as a map, skills that load on demand, and plans checked in.
5. Start where pipelines clearly help, with fleet-wide migrations and routine maintenance (Spotify, Airbnb), then move on to background agents for feature work (Ramp, Stripe).
6. Measure verified outcomes such as review time, human interventions per accepted change, and revert and escaped-defect rates. No leader has published these for agent PRs yet.
7. Test before mandating anything. Run the same real tasks two ways with the same models and environment, once with a lightweight agent loop and a plan where useful, and once with a prescribed multi-stage workflow. If plan approvals are on the table, add a third run that asks for approval only on risky transitions. At Atlassian's HULA, the one deployment that measured a plan gate, engineers approved 82% of plans but only a quarter of the code generated from them. Compare how well each meets the intent, elapsed time and cost, reviewer burden, interventions and post-merge defects, broken down by how ambiguous and risky the tasks are.
